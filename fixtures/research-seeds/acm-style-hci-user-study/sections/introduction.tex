\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A developer who triggers a 40-second build does not sit and watch it. They read
a ticket, answer a message, or start editing a different file, and the
toolchain is then responsible for bringing them back. Every mainstream editor
solves this the same way: a toast in the corner, an animated badge, sometimes a
system notification, delivered the instant the build finishes.

The design rationale is rarely stated but is easy to reconstruct. Build results
are time sensitive, the developer asked for them, and the sooner they know the
sooner they can act. Interruption research has been telling a different story
for two decades. An interruption's cost is not the time it occupies but the
time it takes to rebuild the suspended mental state
afterwards~\citep{brandt2011resumption,ferreiro2014fragmentation}, and that
cost is dominated by whether the interruption forces a shift of visual
attention and a motor action, not by how much information it
carries~\citep{okonjo2016peripheral}.

Applied to compile notifications this predicts something specific and testable:
a notification that appears in the periphery and requires no dismissal should
outperform one that appears centrally and must be clicked away, even though the
two convey identical information. It also predicts that the peripheral
notification should beat no notification at all, since the developer would
otherwise poll the build state manually and pay a self-interruption cost each
time.

We tested both predictions. Thirty-six professional developers each completed
four instrumented editing tasks in a controlled editor, under four compile
notification conditions in a counterbalanced within-subjects design. We
measured resumption lag directly from keystroke and gaze traces rather than
inferring it from task completion time, which is the measurement most prior
work in this specific setting has relied on.

The results support both predictions and are larger than we expected. The
immediate toast condition, which is what nearly every shipping editor does, is
the worst of the four on resumption lag, task completion time, error rate, and
self-reported mental demand. We report the effect sizes, the confidence
intervals, and the one condition where the ordering reverses.

\paragraph*{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item A within-subjects study of compile notification modality with 36
        professional developers and direct instrumentation of resumption lag
        (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item Effect size estimates for four notification designs on four outcomes,
        with the ambient design beating both the immediate toast and the silent
        control (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A boundary condition: the advantage inverts for builds shorter than
        roughly eight seconds, where the developer never disengages and an
        ambient marker is simply missed (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}
