# Tone and structure

A response letter has one job: make it easy for the editor to see that every point was taken seriously. Everything below serves that.

## Structure

Open with two sentences of thanks and a one-paragraph summary of what changed across the whole revision. Then go reviewer by reviewer, point by point, in the reviewers' own order. Never reorder points to put the easy ones first. The editor is reading with the review letter open next to yours.

For each point: the comment verbatim, your response, then the change quoted from the manuscript. The quote is what turns a promise into a fact.

Close with the open points, if there are any, in a short table. Do not bury an unaddressed point in the middle where it looks answered.

## Register

Write it the way you would write to a colleague who did you a favour and got one thing wrong. Direct, specific, unembarrassed.

- "We have added the power calculation to Section 2.3." Good.
- "We are deeply grateful for this insightful comment which has substantially strengthened the manuscript." Filler. Thank them once at the top and then answer.
- "The reviewer has misunderstood our method." Even when true, this reads as defensive. Write "We did not state this clearly. Section 3 now says ..." The reviewer misread it because the text allowed the misreading.

Thank the reviewers once. Repeating it before every answer makes the letter longer and less credible.

## Agreeing

Say what you changed and where. That is the whole reply. Do not explain at length why the reviewer was right.

> **Response.** Agreed. Table 2 now reports the standard deviation alongside each mean.

## Disagreeing

Disagreement is allowed and editors expect some of it. What is not allowed is disagreeing without an argument, or pretending to agree and then changing nothing.

Three sentences: acknowledge the point, give the reason, say what you did instead.

> **Response.** We agree the comparison would be informative, but the baseline requires labelled data that is not available for this cohort. We have added a sentence in Section 5 noting the comparison as future work and narrowed the claim in the abstract from "outperforms existing methods" to "outperforms the two methods evaluated here."

Narrowing a claim is almost always a better answer than arguing. It costs little and it usually resolves the objection.

## Contradictory reviewers

When two reviewers ask for opposite things, do not answer them differently. Pick one course, explain the reasoning once in full, and reference it from the other reply.

> **Response.** Reviewer 1 asked for the derivation to move to an appendix and Reviewer 3 asked for more detail in the main text. We have kept the derivation in the main text and expanded it, because the argument in Section 4 depends on step 3. The full algebra is now in Appendix B for readers who want it. See our response to R1.2.

## Requests you cannot meet

Say so early in the reply, not at the end. Give the real reason: the data does not exist, the equipment is no longer available, the analysis would require a new study, the request is outside the scope the venue allows.

Then offer the largest thing you can do instead: a sensitivity analysis, a limitation, a narrowed claim, a clarifying sentence. An unmet request with a concrete substitute reads as good faith. An unmet request with silence reads as evasion.

Mark it visibly. In the LaTeX template that is `\notchanged`. In the plain-text version it is a **No change.** line. And list it again in the open points table at the end.

## Quoting the manuscript

Quote the revised sentence, not a summary of it. Give the file and the line, or the section and the line number of the revised PDF, whichever the venue expects. If the venue wants a marked-up manuscript as well, say in the letter that one is attached.

Do not quote a passage you have not read back from the file. A response letter that cites a line that does not say what the letter claims is worse than no letter.

## Length

Match the reviewer's investment. A one-line comment gets a one-line answer. A page of methodological objection gets a real paragraph. If the venue caps the response length, cut the prose in each reply. Never cut the number of points you answer.
