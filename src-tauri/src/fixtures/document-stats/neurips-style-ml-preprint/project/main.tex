\begin{abstract}
Instruction fine-tuning corpora are assembled for breadth, but they are almost
always deployed against a narrower target distribution than the one they were
drawn from. We study data selection in that setting and show that the standard
criteria, loss magnitude and gradient norm, select for examples that are hard
rather than for examples that are useful, and that the two diverge sharply once
the pool and the target disagree. \method{} scores a candidate by the cosine
alignment between its sketched per-example gradient and the mean gradient of a
small anchor set drawn from the target domain, then anneals a selection
temperature so that early training concentrates on high-alignment examples and
later training admits the rest. The alignment score costs one extra backward
pass over a 512-dimensional sketch, roughly 3 percent of fine-tuning compute.
Across four shifted benchmarks and two model scales, \method{} reaches the
accuracy of full-pool fine-tuning using 31 percent of the tokens, and beats the
strongest selection baseline by 2.4 points on average. We give an excess-risk
bound that separates the contribution of retained alignment mass from the
contribution of gradient noise, and we report the regime where the method
fails: when the anchor set is smaller than roughly 128 examples the alignment
estimate is dominated by its own variance and selection becomes worse than
random.
\end{abstract}
